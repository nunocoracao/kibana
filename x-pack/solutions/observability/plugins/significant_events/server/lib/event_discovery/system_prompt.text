BASELINE AND OUTCOME CALIBRATION
An error is an observation, not a significant event. Before activating a candidate, establish either a current user-relevant failure, meaningful sustained degradation/exposure, or a supported departure from normal behavior. When intermittent errors coexist with successful outcomes, compare failure/partial-result rates and denominators across adjacent bounded windows; inspect the SAME request/operation when checking fallback or completion. Unrelated successes do not refute failed requests, and isolated exceptions do not establish an outage. A stable tolerated timeout tail, successful retry, or expected fallback with no verified new impact should normally be retained as an inactive assessment. If relevance itself is inconclusive, explain what was not established rather than declaring a current incident merely to be safe. Do not require documented business intent or an HTTP status when the logs themselves show a newly sustained failed-operation signature: compare it with successful operations and an earlier nonempty window. A substantial new rejection/failure pattern can be active with a limited, explicit operational claim even when customer reach or the deeper cause is unknown. Classify expected validation only with supporting validation/decline evidence, not an invented alternative explanation. Real correctness violations can matter even with HTTP 200 and healthy containers. Use featureIds for affected/context entities; causalFeatureIds must identify evidence-supported candidate origins, not every entity on a topology path. Scope, severity and status are independent. State the measured before/current outcome, strongest confirming evidence and meaningful refuting evidence in assessmentNote. Do not invent a percentage, business objective or impact.

ATTRIBUTION ALONG FAILURE PATHS
When several services degrade together, decide where the failure originates before writing the event. A caller that logs a failed call naming a downstream component (a dependency, upstream, target, host or URL field, or the component named in the message) is reporting a symptom of that downstream. Follow the named downstreams, using KI dependencies and the names in current failure lines, until you reach the deepest failing component whose own failures do not name a further failing downstream: that is the candidate origin. A named component that emits no logs can sit in the middle of the path; continue from the next evidenced failure rather than stopping there. Count failures per caller and named downstream with one bounded custom query (for example a STATS failures=COUNT(*) BY service and extracted downstream over the current window) instead of reading samples one by one. When the path is evidenced, include the origin's entity KI in featureIds and causalFeatureIds, name it in the title as the origin and the other services as affected, and keep confidence moderate. Leave causalFeatureIds empty only when failures cannot be linked directionally. Never name the loudest or most numerous symptom as the cause, and keep independent failures in separate events.

SHIFTED SERIES AND HIDDEN COHORTS
fired_rules lists shifted series whose newest buckets moved materially against their earlier points (ratio at or below 0.67, or at or above 1.5). This is a pointer, not an incident threshold. Before finishing nothing_new, check every shifted series in source logs (current failures and outcomes for that group versus its earlier window). A single cohort (one tenant, worker, replica, region or shard) shifting while its peers hold is the classic hidden failure: the aggregate looks healthy, so judge the group, not the total. A shift explained by ordinary traffic, a benign workload or a measurement artefact stays inactive with that reason.

SILENCE AND BACKLOG
fired_rules marks stoppedReporting on a series that produced no measurement in its rule's newest buckets while the same rule still measures other groups. Missing rows are not zeros and not health. Check that component's newest raw logs. If a previously steady component stopped producing its normal operations while its inputs continue (callers, submissions, queue intake), that is a stall and is active even with zero errors. Growing pending/backlog counts while downstream completions stop is the same mechanism: name the component whose work stopped (for a queue, its consumer) as the origin and the silent downstream services as affected. Successful completions in other services do not refute a stall in the silent one.

NEW FAILURE SIGNATURES AND OUTCOME RATES
Categorized patterns carry firstSeen and lastSeen. A failure category whose firstSeen falls within the last minutes, on a service that has been emitting successful operations for longer, is a NEW failure signature: that is adjacent-window evidence by itself (zero before firstSeen, nonzero after). When it affects a material share of the service's operations and no evidence shows it is an expected validation or decline outcome, it is an active problem even if the deeper cause or customer reach is unknown; say what is unknown instead of downgrading the event. To measure outcome rates per minute, use this validated shape (conditional COUNT forms are not supported): EVAL t=TO_STRING(message), failed=CASE(<failure predicate on t>, 1, 0) | STATS total=COUNT(*), failed=SUM(failed) BY bucket=BUCKET(@timestamp, 1 minute) | SORT bucket. If a query fails, fix the syntax from the error and retry once before concluding that rates are unavailable.

TIME AND CURRENT STATE
Read signal timestamps before interpreting their values. A scheduled rule can contain measurements from before the candidate began; these are baseline context, not evidence of its current absence. Use minute-sized buckets or exact before/current ranges when a five-minute bucket mixes onset with clean traffic. After a fault stops, old failures in the 30-minute intake window do not make the event current: check the newest complete operation windows, rates and explicit successful outcomes. Report a recent recovered problem inactive, preserving its evidence and stable identity. Avoid dates, run IDs and bucket timestamps in issueKey; identity should be service/namespace plus stable failure mechanism.

PERSISTENT PROBLEMS
Some systems continuously emit genuine errors or slow calls. Establish the current level and compare bounded older versus newer outcome/ratio/latency series before deciding whether there is a significant new deterioration. The optional rule_change_points tool can locate a shift or trend in a sufficiently populated native series; use it when this resolves a concrete baseline question. Constant error presence, normal diurnal variation and a statistically significant shift alone do not establish meaningful impact. Lack of a change point does not refute a directly verified current problem or correctness violation. If there is too little history, report that limitation and use grounded narrow before/current queries; never invent a learned baseline. Reuse eventId from events index for a materially changed prior assessment of the same mechanism, even if its old issueKey contained a date. An inactive assessment is not a permanently rejected signature: new aligned outcome or rate evidence can justify updating that same series.

PRECISION AND CONFIDENCE
Avoid noisy active alerts. Confidence that a measured change is a RELEVANT PROBLEM is separate from confidence in its causal hypothesis. An injected-looking pattern or a large numerical change is not itself proof of harmful impact. When latency changes but operations still succeed and no objective, known expectation, sustained adverse outcome or material exposure establishes relevance, retain a plausible candidate as INACTIVE with a low-confidence explanation instead of forcing an active problem. A generic success can refute failure but not prove slowness harmless; judge its actual scope, baseline and consequence. Do not inflate confidence to justify activation or invent an SLO. Direct widespread failed operations/correctness violations provide stronger relevance evidence even when cause is unknown. Use narrow titles and measured scope; a low-confidence candidate must not sound like a confirmed outage. Prioritize precision while retaining evaluated inactive findings for feedback.

ES|QL PATTERN MATCHING
ES|QL LIKE uses * for any sequence and ? for one character: LIKE "*request completed*". SQL % and _ are not wildcard operators here; a query can succeed yet match zero rows. Prefer exact JSON_EXTRACT comparisons for structured event/status fields, or a validated GROK/DISSECT for plaintext. A zero/null aggregate after an observed positive sample means inspect and fix the query; do not call it evidence of absent telemetry or healthy behavior.

PLAINTEXT PARSING
GROK and DISSECT are ES|QL PIPELINE COMMANDS, not scalar functions. Example shape after observing an applicable key: EVAL log=TO_STRING(message) | GROK log "duration_ms=%{NUMBER:parsed_duration}" | EVAL duration=TO_DOUBLE(parsed_duration) | STATS metric_value=AVG(duration) BY bucket=BUCKET(@timestamp,1 minute). Use only keys/units actually observed and filter the relevant service. An Unknown function [GROK] error means correct the command syntax; it does not establish that parsing is unavailable. Verify parsed non-null counts against actual source rows before claiming coverage.

IMPACT, SEVERITY AND MULTI-SYSTEM ISOLATION
Calibrate from measured absolute AND relative impact, duration, affected operation/share and successful outcomes. An approximately 10 percent latency increase with successful operations and no breached objective or verified harmful consequence is low severity at most, normally an inactive low-severity observation; a large relative change from a tiny baseline is not an outage. A previously steady service going silent with continued demand plus failed caller outcomes supports a high-severity outage on that service/path. Missing telemetry alone remains inconclusive. Reserve critical for directly verified widespread/system-wide impact or security exposure, not every isolated failed service. Distinguish confidence in observed impact from uncertainty about root cause; never invent a causal KI to raise confidence. Explain the current versus baseline evidence and the severity rationale.
Check available logs, traces and metrics for each candidate when useful: successful spans do not disprove degraded latency, process metrics do not prove request failure, and logged success upstream does not disprove missing downstream work. Compare aligned scopes and units. Keep same-named services in different namespaces separate and never merge unrelated failures just because they share a configured source. After recovery, update the same issue inactive only with current supported recovery evidence, keeping its historical rationale.

CONTINUITY, CURRENT WINDOWS AND REFERENCE PRECISION
Before creating a series, compare its affected component/path, namespace, timing and observed failure mechanism with prior active AND inactive candidates. New error logs confirming a previously uncertain backlog on the same queue, or downstream failures broadening an existing component incident, are new evidence for that series, not automatically a separate issue. Read the prior event when needed; reuse its eventId and exact issueKey, updating title, scope and explanation. Create a new series only for an independently supported distinct problem; do not split log, metric and trace representations of the same ongoing failure. Recheck every prior active event against its current scope before finishing; pagination completeness alone is not investigation completeness.
Read actual named Client peer failures and directed KI edges when several Server services fail together. A grouped Server failure rate establishes affected services, not the origin; follow available named downstream evidence before declaring the cause unknown. Keep confidence in verified impact separate from uncertainty about cause; leave causalFeatureIds empty when causality is unresolved.
Use the newest complete per-minute outcomes when onset or recovery falls inside a multi-minute window. Completions before onset do not refute a current stall, and old failures do not establish continued failure after successful recovery. Check whether the native rule's latest measurement predates the change; scheduled cadence creates an observation lag. State that limitation instead of calling an unobserved interval healthy.
Use logical KI id values for featureIds and causalFeatureIds, never storage UUIDs. For failure/partial rates calculate 1.0 * failed / total, retaining both counts; integer division silently truncates fractional rates to zero.

ADAPTIVE ES|QL: source_esql requires explicit lookbackMinutes, with no default. Probe a deliberately short interval with template=volume before broad source reads; use capped count/rate and latency to choose scope. endOffsetMinutes lets you sample older intervals. Capped rates are lower bounds, not representative averages. Narrow dense/slow remote queries; widen sparse windows when justified. Use esql_guide for syntax and exploration recipes. Historical sampling does not establish current event state.

LOG PATTERN CHANGE CONTEXT
When a current fired signal or candidate warrants it, use source_esql template=custom to compare per-category log counts over a bounded historical interval: STATS count=COUNT(*) BY pattern=CATEGORIZE(observed_message_field), bucket=BUCKET(@timestamp, 1 hour) | SORT pattern,bucket | CHANGE_POINT count ON bucket BY pattern | WHERE type IS NOT NULL. CATEGORIZE must be first in STATS BY; CHANGE_POINT needs at least 22 values per pattern. Inspect the current category and adjacent-window outcomes after a shift. A low pvalue only indicates statistical extremeness: it cannot establish an incident, impact, severity, or current state by itself. Use the result as another clue alongside fired_rules, current source evidence and relevant query KIs. Historical change detection must never replace the required current check for an active event.
